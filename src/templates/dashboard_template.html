Churn is a first-year, month-to-month, fiber problem

An analysis of 7,043 telecom customers: where they leave, what keeps them, how long they stay, and what to do about it first.

Who leaves

New customers on month-to-month plans, especially fiber customers who pay by electronic check and have no security or tech support.

What keeps people

Term contracts, security and tech support add-ons, automatic payment, and time. Gender, phone service and streaming use make no real difference.

What to do first

Convert month-to-month customers to term plans, bundle support into fiber, move payers to autopay, and fix the first 90 days.

Customers leave early, and the risk drops fast

A customer is about seven times more likely to leave in any given month during months 1 to 3 than during year three. Three in ten churners are gone within 90 days.

Commitment and support services are the strongest retention levers

Controlling for the other factors at once, a two-year contract cuts the odds of churn by 74%. Security and tech support each cut them by about a third, independent of contract, tenure and price.

Gender and phone service make no real difference. Among internet customers, streamers pay about $86 a month against $61 and churn slightly less (30% versus 34%), so streaming is not a risk flag.

Month-to-month customers are retained for about half as long

Over a five-year window the average month-to-month customer stays 33 months. A one-year customer stays 57. That gap is worth roughly $1,500 in revenue per customer, even though month-to-month customers pay slightly more per month.

Why customers likely leave: risk factors that stack up

The data has no stated churn reasons, so these are inferred from what churned customers have in common. Customers with none of the five risk factors churn at 2.5%. Those with all five churn at 74%.

Where to start: five plays aimed at the largest churn gaps

Each play targets a segment where the data shows a large churn gap. Estimates assume only part of the gap is caused by the factor, so each play should launch as a controlled test.

1

Move month-to-month customers onto 12-month plans

Offer a price lock plus a free security trial in exchange for a 12-month term. Start with the 1,994 month-to-month customers in their first year, and prioritize fiber. Month-to-month churn is 43% against 11% on one-year plans, and a one-year term lowers the odds of churn by about half after controlling for everything else.

Target3,875 month-to-month customers
Success metricTerm conversion at 90 days, 12-month retention
EffortMediumneeds margin review on the offer
2

Bundle security and tech support into fiber

1,765 fiber customers have neither service, and 55% of them churn. Fiber customers with at least one churn at 24%. Offer a three-month free bundle at sign-up and at month 2, and measure how many keep it once billing starts. Check with legal that the trial is clearly disclosed.

TargetFiber customers without security or tech support
Success metricAttach rate, 6-month churn versus control
EffortLow to mediummostly offer design and support capacity
3

Get electronic check payers onto autopay

Electronic check customers churn at 45%, against 15% to 17% for automatic payers. Among month-to-month customers it is 54% versus 33%. Offer a small one-time bill credit for switching, and make autopay the default at checkout. Pull payment-failure data too, to see how much of this is involuntary churn from failed payments.

Target1,850 month-to-month e-check customers first
Success metricShare on autopay, churn versus control
EffortLowbilling flow change plus a credit
4

Fix the first 90 days, starting with fiber

597 customers, 32% of all churners, leave within three months. Add scheduled touchpoints: a setup check in week 1, a speed and usage check around day 14, a call at day 30, and the term-plan offer at day 60. In parallel, run a fiber diagnostic with exit surveys, outage and ticket data, and competitor pricing. Fiber churn stays high even after controlling for contract and support, and the cause is not in this dataset.

TargetAll new customers, fiber first
Success metricChurn in months 1 to 3
EffortMediumneeds ops and support involvement
5

Score churn risk and give the save team a short list

A gradient-boosting model reaches 0.85 AUC on held-out customers. Its top 20% of scores contained 49% of churners, with a 65% churn rate against 27% overall. Use it to trigger outreach and match each offer to the driver: contract, support or payment. Before relying on it, rebuild it on dated snapshots so it predicts the next 90 days rather than describing past tenure.

Suggested sequence

Weeks 1 to 4

  • Pull payment-failure and ticket data to split voluntary from involuntary churn
  • Launch the autopay credit test (play 3)
  • Set up holdout groups for every offer

Weeks 5 to 8

  • Launch the fiber support bundle (play 2) and the term-plan offer (play 1)
  • Start fiber exit surveys
  • Design the 90-day touchpoints

Weeks 9 to 12

  • Roll out onboarding touchpoints (play 4)
  • Read first results and scale the winners
  • Build the dated risk model (play 5)

What this dataset can and cannot answer

  • Data. The Telco customer churn dataset: 7,043 customers, one snapshot, 21 fields covering services, contract, billing and demographics. It is a sample dataset, so rerun the analysis on your production data before committing budget.
  • Churn and tenure. Churn means the customer left in the latest period. Tenure is months before leaving for churners and months so far for active customers. Retention curves use the Kaplan–Meier method to handle the active customers properly. Eleven customers with zero tenure have blank total charges and were counted as $0.
  • Lifetime value. Average months retained within 60 months, multiplied by the segment's average monthly bill. It is a revenue view and does not include margin or acquisition cost.
  • Drivers. Odds ratios come from a logistic regression on tenure, monthly charge, services, contract, payment, billing and demographics, with intervals from 200 bootstrap resamples.
  • Risk model. Gradient boosting on a 75/25 split (1,761 test customers). The 0.85 AUC is probably optimistic because tenure partly encodes the outcome in a single snapshot. Validate on forward-looking data.
  • Not causal. Every gap here is observational. Customers who choose term contracts or buy support may differ in ways the data cannot see, which is why the impact estimates show a half-gap case, and why each play should be tested against a holdout.
  • Missing. Churn reasons, support tickets, usage, price changes, calendar dates and competitor activity. Adding them would sharpen the fiber diagnosis most.
